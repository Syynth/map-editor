Five Modes Deep

The prototype answered its questions. Its chrome was never designed — it was accreted. This is what an artist meets when they try to raise a hill, paint it, and put a tree on it, and what the chrome should look like instead.

5levels of mode between "I want to raise this" and a brush stroke: tool, sub-mode, verb, stroke kind, brush
110pxwidth of the entire tile palette — a sheet thumbnail and a paragraph, for the artist's core task
12controls shown at one level when a tree is selected; seven of them are billboard mechanics
24×outliner rows called tree, rock, bush — every object named by its sprite

The frame

Three things change at the frame level and everything else follows from them. Camera stops being a tool — orbit and pan are always available, and its bounds are a property of the level, not a mode. The left panel becomes a rail and a context bar: three tools you can name from their icons, and one strip under the top bar that holds only what the active tool needs. The inspector loses its tabs — Selection, Level, and Objects stack as collapsible sections, so the coverage warnings live on the objects they concern instead of two tabs away.

Play is the one action an artist takes a hundred times a session. It gets the accent, and nothing else in the top bar does.

Sculpt

The verbs are already right — raise, flatten, ramp, water are the four things a heightfield artist does. What is wrong is the path to them and the absence of lower. There is no lower verb; the artist has to know a modifier exists, and nothing on screen says so.

TodayPress 1, then Tab to reach Sculpt, then choose a verb in one segmented control, a stroke kind in another, then set size and shape — the same five decisions every time the artist returns from painting.
KeepUndo naming its edit ("raise, 14 cells"). Rect and Fill as stroke kinds. Alt-click as the universal eyedropper. The one-tile-one-unit grid.

Paint

Painting is what the brief says the artist came for, and it has the least designed surface in the editor. The artist thinks in materials — grass, dirt, stone, water — not in the sixteen autotile variants the sheet lays out for the mesher. The palette should show materials as large named swatches, put the current one where the eye already is, and keep the sheet itself behind a door for the day the artist wants to check a variant.

TodayA 110-pixel thumbnail of the whole template sheet, a Load PNG button, and four lines of prose explaining the row layout. Picking a tile means alt-clicking one that is already on the map.
KeepTint as a paint verb. The sheet-mismatch warnings on load. Dormant-paint survival — it should get a visible count when it is non-zero, and be silent otherwise.

Objects

The objects tool is a dropdown of eight words. Sprites are images; show them. And when the artist selects a tree, the first thing they see should be the four things they might change — name, sprite, how it faces, size — with the billboard machinery folded away under one honest heading. Hysteresis is a real setting and it should exist, one level down.

TodaySprite: [tree ▾] in the left panel. On selection, twelve controls at one level: Name, Display, Scale, Rotation, Anchored, Facings, Mirror, Back side, Transition, Duration, Hysteresis, Hinge.
KeepAuto as the default display mode — the coverage readout proved it does most of the work. The one-click "Billboard it" fix, which moves onto the object row.

Coverage, and what stops being a tool

The coverage readout is the most useful thing the prototype produced: it turns "should the camera rotate?" into a count of images the artist would have to draw. It is two clicks away in a tab, next to a copy of the camera controls. Its warnings belong on the objects — a rose dot in the list, a chip in the top bar with the count, a "fix" that applies the one-click billboard to every flagged object at once — and its explanation belongs in the Level section beside the camera bounds it depends on.

TodayA Coverage tab that also repeats the camera rig. A Camera tool whose left panel is empty and whose controls live in Properties, unlike every other tool.
KeepThe readout's exact language — "fixing every flag costs about 3 more images" is the sentence that makes the trade-off real. The yaw-range presets (Free / 4 detents / Narrow / Fixed).

With free rotation, 3 of 24 objects read wrong from some angle. Fixing every flag costs about 3 more images to draw, or one click to billboard them. 0% of cliff faces are ever hidden at this range; narrowing to 20–70° would hide half of them.

What the prototype got right

None of this is a rewrite of the editing model. The verbs are right. One tile is one unit, heights are half-tiles, cliff paint survives sculpting — those are the decisions that make the tools feel solid, and they are all kept. So is the keyboard: 1 2 3 for tools, Tab between sub-modes becomes unnecessary once Sculpt and Paint are tools of their own, and every verb gets a single key on the home row. The tour, the coverage math, and the undo naming are the parts of the chrome worth being proud of; the proposal moves them to where the artist is looking.

How this phase runs

React

You look at these, say what's wrong, what's missing, and what you'd never do. The rail-versus-panel question and "camera is not a tool" are the two calls that shape everything else — settle those first.

Pick one surface

The Paint palette is the highest-value, lowest-risk place to start: it touches one tool and it's the artist's core task. The frame comes second, once the actor migration has landed and the UI is reading from the host.

Build it together

Mantine is the substrate (#12) and `packages/ui` owns the vocabulary, so each surface is a set of primitives plus one panel. We build one, drive it, adjust, and move to the next. No waves, no tickets — just the editor getting better.

The viewports in these mockups are drawn stand-ins, not renders. Everything else — the type, the spacing, the palette, the copy in the status bar — is what the proposal actually looks like at 1440 × 900.
